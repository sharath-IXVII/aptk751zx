[STEP BY STEP]

So, how was cuneiform written? To understand this, we have to go back to ancient Mesopotamia, the land between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers, roughly 3400 BCE. This is where the Sumerians, the people who invented writing, first pressed clay tablets.

The writing surface was wet clay. Not the hardened pottery we think of today, but soft, malleable mud. The scribe would take a reed, cut and sharpened into a broad chisel-shaped tip, and press it into the clay. "Cuneiform" means 'wedge-shaped,' referring to the signs created, but the tool itself was designed to incise the clay. When the scribe pressed this chisel-shaped tip into the wet clay, it did not simply displace the clay or push the mud aside. It incised a groove or a wedge-shaped mark. Because the clay was wet, the mark stayed. The scribe could then rotate the tablet, press another wedge, and build up a whole sentence.

Now, I know what you are thinking. 'Isn't that just drawing?' It is not. Drawing is about creating an image that looks like the object. Cuneiform is about creating signs that represent sounds or concepts. But the physical act—pressing a stick into clay—is the same in principle, though the tool's purpose was incising, not shading.

Let me give you an everyday analogy. Imagine you have a ball of play-dough. You want to write your name on it, but you don't have a pen. You decide to use a sharpened stick or a needle. You press the point into the dough to create a dot, or drag it to create a line. That is the basic action. The 'fork' analogy often seen in popular media is physically misleading; a fork cuts or tears, whereas the reed stylus was used to draw precise linear signs into the wet mud. The scribe was not just 'pushing' the clay; they were creating specific geometric shapes.

But here is the part most people get wrong. We often see pictures of cuneiform and think the scribes were writing in straight lines like we do on a page. They were not. Because the clay tablet was portable, the scribes often wrote in vertical columns, starting from the top and working down. The direction of the writing changed over time and by culture. Early on, the writing was typically vertical, arranged in columns. Later, as the script evolved, signs could be written in horizontal rows or right-to-left, but vertical columns remained the standard for millennia. The stick stayed the same, but the way the scribe held the tablet and the direction of the writing changed completely.

[MYTHS AND MISTAKES]

Let's talk about a common myth. Many people believe that cuneiform was used to write stories about gods from the very beginning. While religion was a huge part of Mesopotamian life, the very first cuneiform tablets were not 'receipts' in the modern commercial sense. The earliest tablets are accounting records from the Uruk period. They tracked amounts of barley, quantities of wool, and numbers of livestock. Writing began as bookkeeping and administrative record-keeping for temple economies. It was only centuries later that scribes began using the same system to write poetry, myths, and laws. So, if you think cuneiform is only about epic tales of creation, remember: it started with the bureaucratic needs of the first city-states.

Another mistake people make is thinking that every wedge meant the same thing. Cuneiform was not a simple alphabet where one symbol equals one letter. It was a complex logo-syllabic system. A single sign could represent a whole word, like 'king' or 'god.' But it could also be combined with other signs to spell out a sound, like 'ba' or 'ta.' It was a mix of picture-based ideas and phonetic sounds. The scribe had to know hundreds of these signs to be fluent. Think of it less like emojis and more like a complex system where a single symbol can have multiple readings depending on context.

[PRO TIPS AND CLOSING]

For those of you inspired to try it yourself, here is a pro tip. If you want to experiment with cuneiform, use air-dry clay and a bamboo skewer. The skewer needs to be cut or sharpened to a broad chisel-shaped edge. Do not use a fine point. Press the chisel edge into the clay to create incised lines or wedge shapes. Do not drag the skewer across the clay like you are writing with a pencil, or you will just tear the clay. Press, lift, and rotate the clay. That is the rhythm of the ancient scribe.

Let's do a quick recap. Cuneiform was written by pressing a reed stylus into wet clay. The tool was cut to a broad chisel shape to incise wedge marks. It started as administrative accounting for temple economies, evolved into a complex mix of sounds and ideas, and was predominantly written in vertical columns, read from top to bottom. The tool was simple, but the impact was massive. It proved that humans could record their thoughts, and that record has lasted for five thousand years.
